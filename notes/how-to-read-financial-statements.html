How to Read Financial Statements

Lochlan de Wilde · 8 November 2026

A financial statement gives a sense of a company’s financial health. Knowing how to read one is essential to GRAM’s work.

When assessing risk, you have to look at a company’s health and answer three basic questions:

  • Is the company making money?
  • Does it have a lot of debt?
  • How is the cash flow?

The answers are built into the financial statements. Every company has them, so they are worth learning to read.

Terms

  • Assets: what a company owns. There are two types, current and non-current.
  • Current assets: can easily be turned into cash within the company’s fiscal year.
    • For example: cash, digital currency, an uncashed cheque, money customers owe.
  • Non-current assets: not liquid; they cannot easily be turned into cash.
    • For example: an office building, machinery, long-term investments in stocks or bonds.
  • Liabilities: anything the company owes, such as bills, payments to clients and pensions.
  • Shareholder equity: the part of the business the owners actually own.
    • For example: you start a bakery with Paul, Jim and Gary, and you each own 25%.

The formula

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholder equity

Everything a company owns had to be paid for somehow: either with borrowed money (liabilities) or with the owners’ money (equity). So both sides always match.

Say you buy a bakery worth CHF 100,000. You put in 40,000 of your own money and take a 60,000 mortgage from the bank.

AssetsLiabilitiesEquity
100,00060,00040,000

100,000 = 60,000 + 40,000

Now say you put in another 10,000 of your own money to pay back part of the mortgage. Liabilities fall to 50,000 and equity rises to 50,000. The bakery is still worth 100,000, but you own more of it.

Why it matters for GRAM

Knowing the basics lets us understand and analyse companies, and judge their financial health. Without financial statements, investors would not know what is going on inside the companies they own.
